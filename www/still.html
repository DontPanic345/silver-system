The still

Juniper standing in water, in a pot held at 366 K, and a cold receiver on the other side of a wall with a gap in it. Gin comes out of the far end. Nothing here is a brewing mechanic: the pot is warm, so the botanicals ferment; wash boils at 351.5 K and water at 373.15 K, and both follow a vapour-pressure curve read off those same two numbers, so at 366 K the air over the pot fills with several times as much spirit as steam. That gap is the whole of distilling — and because water does evaporate below its boiling point, some water comes over too and the receiver collects a little of it under the gin, as a real pot still's does.

The run

Mean temperature—

  1. Mash — juniper touching water above 310 K becomes wash. One row of the reaction table; nothing names juniper.
  2. Boil — wash gives off spirit vapour, fast where it touches air and wholesale where it boils beneath the surface.
  3. Cross — spirit vapour is denser than air (46 g/mol against 29), so it lies on top of the wash and creeps sideways through the gap rather than rising.
  4. Condense — against the cold floor and wall it forms dew, which falls as drops of gin and pools.
  5. Drink — the two gnomes started thirsty.

Boiling off a gram of spirit costs 846 J and gives it back on condensing, so a still is a heat pump with a hole in it. The hob and the cold receiver are declared boundaries — every joule they move goes on the ledger, which is why the residuals above stay at floating-point noise while the still runs.
